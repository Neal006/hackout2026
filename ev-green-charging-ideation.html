Hackathon ideation · EV charging × renewable grid supply

Noonshift

A deadline-based charging scheduler that moves flexible EV charging into the hours the grid is actually clean — starting with the one place drivers already park for eight hours in daylight: the workplace lot.

Working name — rename freely Research date: 11 Sep 2026 All references opened and cross-checked (see §13)
Read this first

The problem is not that grids are dirty. It is that EV charging happens at the wrong time by default. 72% of home sessions start the moment the plug goes in; fewer than 26% of drivers ever schedule. Meanwhile California threw away 3.4 TWh of mostly-solar electricity in 2024 because nobody was there to use it at noon.

Root cause: nobody captures the one piece of information that unlocks flexibility — when the driver needs the car — and the products that do exist optimise overnight home charging for utilities, broadcast one “green” signal to everyone (which the newest research shows can backfire), and never make the result legible to the person plugging in.

Solution: ask one question at plug-in (“When do you leave?”), let a site-level optimiser allocate power across every connector every five minutes to minimise marginal carbon and tariff cost inside the site’s power limit, guarantee the car is ready on time, and show the driver one honest number: money and CO₂ saved versus charging immediately.

ev.energy, Optiwatt, WeaveGrid and Octopus optimise overnight home charging for the utility. Noonshift optimises the daytime workplace lot for the site owner — 8 hours of dwell, ~8 kWh of need, and a midday solar surplus the grid is currently curtailing.

01

The problem statement

›

Given: As EV adoption grows, charging stations draw power regardless of whether the grid is running on renewables or fossil fuels at that moment. Build a platform that helps charging network operators schedule and price sessions to align with high-renewable periods, and gives drivers real-time visibility into the greenness of their session. Users: operators, drivers, grid operators.

What “aligning with renewables” physically means

You cannot pick which electrons you get. A charger draws from the whole grid mix at that instant. The only lever a charging operator has is when and how hard each connector draws. “Green charging” is therefore a timing claim: you moved a kWh from a moment when adding load would have spun up a gas turbine to a moment when it would have absorbed solar that was otherwise curtailed.

That reframing matters. It means the platform is a scheduler under constraints — not a dashboard, and not a certificate generator.

02

The root cause — where, why, how, what

›

Where the mismatch lives

In time

Solar peaks 09–15. Default charging starts at plug-in — 17:00–23:00 at home, exactly when the evening ramp is met by gas. CAISO must keep gas online through the day for reliability and ramping, then curtails solar at noon [6].

In place

Most managed-charging products run at home, overnight, via utility programs. The site type with the most idle plugged-in hours during the solar window — workplaces, campuses, depots — is where the products aren’t [2].

In the price signal

Retail off-peak windows were designed for a different grid. Octopus Intelligent Go’s off-peak is 23:30–05:30 [22]. Price ≠ carbon in most tariffs. (California’s commercial BEV rate is the exception that proves the point: its super-off-peak is 09:00–14:00 [12].)

In the carbon signal

Most “% renewable” displays use the average mix. A decision to add load is governed by the marginal generator. And when a large fleet follows the same marginal signal, the signal itself breaks [3].

Why it persists — four root causes

  1. Default behaviour is “charge now”. 72% of home sessions begin immediately at plug-in; fewer than 26% involve any deliberate scheduling, even under time-of-use tariffs (Rivian telematics, 2023 data) [1]. Behaviour-change products fail here by construction.
  2. Nobody captures flexibility. The single fact that turns a car into a shiftable load — departure time — is not asked for at most chargers. Without it an operator cannot delay anything without risk of stranding someone, so it delays nothing.
  3. Incentives point the wrong way. Utilities fund overnight home programs; workplace chargers bill per kWh or per hour, so flexibility has no price. Operators are paid to deliver energy, not to deliver it at the right moment.
  4. The “green signal” approach has a scale ceiling. Martin, Powell & Rajagopal (Nature Communications, Dec 2025) show that managing charging with average or marginal emissions factors can increase emissions when the signal is noisy, when too many EVs follow the same signal, or when coal responds. Plain marginal signals turn net-negative beyond ~1.5 M EVs in their Western-US model; they advise against them beyond ~500 k vehicles [3]. Every incumbent broadcasts one signal.

How it shows up

  • CAISO curtailed 3.4 TWh in 2024 (+29% year on year; 93% solar; concentrated in spring midday). Solar covers almost half of demand 08:00–16:00, yet gas stays online for evening ramp [6]. Batteries are eating into it — curtailment fell from 13% to 11.5% of solar generation in early 2025 — but absolute volume still rose [7].
  • Under forecast adoption, peak net demand in the Western Interconnection rises up to 25%; home-centred, locally-optimised charging strains the grid, while shifting to daytime charging reduces storage need, excess renewable generation, ramping and emissions [2].
  • A workplace car dwells 8–10 h and needs 7–10 kWh — about 1–1.5 h of charging at 7 kW. Roughly 85% of the plugged-in time is idle flexibility that nobody uses [18].

What must change (the design brief that falls out)

  • Capture the deadline at plug-in, with a zero-effort default.
  • Put the optimiser where the flexibility is: daytime, long-dwell, multi-connector sites.
  • Optimise centrally per site under the site’s real power limit — never broadcast a signal for thousands of independent actors to chase.
  • Make the outcome visible and honest to the driver, and bankable to the site owner.
03

The proposed solution

›

Noonshift is a charging-operator-side scheduling layer plus a thin driver app. It sits between the charging management system and the chargers (via OCPP), takes one input from the driver, and decides every five minutes how much power each connector gets.

The scheduler, in plain terms

Time is cut into 5-minute slots. For every plugged-in car i and slot t the optimiser picks a power level pi,t. It minimises:

Σ_i Σ_t  p[i,t]·Δ · ( w_c · MOER[t]  +  w_$ · tariff[t] )      # carbon + energy cost
 + w_peak · P_over                                           # exceeding the site's tariff kW block
 + M · Σ_i shortfall[i]                                      # (large M) never fail, degrade gracefully
 + μ · Σ_t committed[t] · Σ_i p[i,t]                          # multi-site only: avoid piling into one trough

subject to, for every car i and slot t in [arrival_i, departure_i):
  Σ_t p[i,t]·Δ + shortfall[i]  ≥  E_need[i]                  # energy by deadline (elastic)
  Σ_{τ≤t} p[i,τ]·Δ  ≥  α · E_need[i] · (t−arrival_i)/(departure_i−arrival_i)
                                                             # progress floor, α≈0.5: fairness + early-unplug safety
  p[i,t] = p_max[i]  for the last 30 min before departure_i while energy remains   # unconditional sprint
  0 ≤ p[i,t] ≤ p_max[i]  (p_max set on the charger by the operator, outside the optimiser)
  Σ_i p[i,t]  ≤  P_site − P_building[t]                      # live site limit incl. building load
  Σ_i p[i,t] − P_over  ≤  P_block                            # tariff subscription block
  • Size: 40 connectors × 288 slots ≈ 11.5 k variables. HiGHS (via scipy.optimize.linprog) solves this in well under a second. Re-solving every 5 min — and immediately on any event (new plug-in, Boost, deadline edit) — is what absorbs forecast error and battery taper.
  • Minimum current: EVs cannot charge below 6 A (IEC 61851). After solving, allocations between 0 and 6 A are rounded up to 6 A rather than paused, because some EVs do not resume after a pause.
  • Why elastic: an oversubscribed lot (too many late arrivals, small site limit) has no exact solution. The shortfall slack lets the solver return the best proportional plan instead of crashing, and the affected drivers get a revised ETA plus the Boost option.
  • Why “progress floor”: one constraint does two jobs. It formalises fairness (no car starves while others charge) and protects drivers who leave early (they always have at least α of the pro-rata energy).

Who pays

  • The site owner — the payer the incumbents don’t serve. PG&E’s Business EV rate has a 09:00–14:00 super-off-peak, a 16:00–21:00 peak, and bills by subscribed kW blocks instead of demand charges [12]. The scheduler’s carbon term and cost term pull the same direction, and “stay inside the kW block” is a real dollar. The demo prints the site’s monthly bill delta vs charge-immediately.
  • Utility managed-charging payments where they exist (precedent: Ava Community Energy pays Optiwatt users $75 + $25/yr; target 5,000 vehicles) [21].
  • Drivers pay nothing extra by default. Green is the wrapper, not the price. Boost is the only premium.
  • Later: demand-response revenue through the OpenADR endpoint; hourly-matched clean-energy reporting for corporate fleets (EnergyTag granular certificates — first issuers accredited June 2025 [15]).

05

What is genuinely new — and what we don’t claim

›

Every individual ingredient exists somewhere. The combination, the placement, and the honesty are the contribution.

Defensible firsts (we found no product doing these — stated as “not found”, not “does not exist”)

  1. Deadline-elastic, marginal-carbon-aware, site-constrained allocation at public/workplace chargers. Incumbent CPO platforms (Driivz, Ampcontrol, Monta) optimise site load and cost, some with a CO₂ preference; utility programs (ev.energy, Optiwatt, WeaveGrid) optimise single home cars via telematics. None we found do all three — deadline, marginal carbon, hard site limit — in one solver at a multi-connector daytime site.
  2. Herding-aware by architecture. The Dec-2025 finding that broadcast signals backfire is new enough that no incumbent has responded to it. Noonshift never broadcasts; multi-site plans see each other’s committed load.
  3. The counterfactual receipt. Not “you charged on 62% renewables” (an average that would have been true if you’d done nothing) but “versus charging at plug-in, this session avoided X kg CO₂ and $Y” — the only number that measures what the product did.

What we explicitly do not claim

  • Not “100% renewable charging”. Electrons are not attributable; this is a timing claim with an estimated impact.
  • Not a solution to grid-scale herding on our own. That needs an aggregator and a grid operator’s dispatch model (the paper’s full method). We avoid causing it and leave the hook in.
  • Not a certificate. The receipt is an estimate from a third-party marginal model. Accredited hourly certificates (EnergyTag) are a roadmap integration, not a feature.
  • Not for DC fast charging. A driver waiting 30 minutes has no flexibility to sell; we show greenness there for awareness only.

10

Limitations — stated plainly

›
  • Impact numbers are estimates. They depend on a third-party marginal emissions model. Marginal signals are noisy (a small demand change can double the signal when the marginal unit flips from gas to coal [3]). We label them; we do not certify them.
  • One operator cannot fix grid-scale herding. We avoid causing it and expose the hooks; the full solution requires aggregators and grid operators to share dispatch models.
  • Savings depend on the tariff. Where off-peak is overnight and the site is a workplace, cost and carbon may conflict; the weights w_c, w_$ make the trade-off explicit rather than hidden.
  • Signal quality varies by region. California and GB are excellent; many grids (including India’s regional zones) have coarser or modelled data. The adapter pattern helps; it does not create data.
  • The demo uses simulated chargers (OCPP 1.6J, the same messages a real EVSE exchanges). Real hardware adds firmware quirks we have not met yet.
  • Adoption at real sites is unproven. The zero-decision default is designed for the 72% who never schedule; whether site owners buy is the commercial question the bill-delta demo is meant to answer.
  • No bidirectional (V2G) charging. ISO 15118-20 / OCPP 2.1 are left as extension points.
  • Attribution is temporal, not physical. “Green charging” means moving load to cleaner hours; it never means specific renewable electrons.
11

Pressure test — five-advisor council and peer review

›

The draft solution was run through five independent perspectives (contrarian, first-principles, expansionist, outsider, executor), each reviewed anonymously by the others, then synthesised. What changed as a result is marked.

Where they agreed

  • Anti-herding cohort math is three orders of magnitude away from a 40-connector lot. → Cut to an honest slide; kept as architecture (no broadcast) and a multi-site penalty term.
  • Three pricing lanes (“Green Flex / Standard / Boost”) were a liability: the green lane was the slowest lane, contradicting the deferral-aversion evidence, and they manufactured the very coordination problem the cohort math then solved. → One question, one Boost toggle.
  • “When do you leave?” is the strongest asset. Keep it in front; bury the math.
  • “Fairness” isn’t in the product until it is a constraint. → Progress-floor constraint.
  • No payer had been named. → Site owner on the PG&E BEV rate; utility programs second; drivers pay nothing extra.

Where they clashed

  • Transparency vs control. If the system already picked the optimal action, does the driver need to see carbon at all? Resolution: the driver sees one outcome number (saved $ and CO₂), not the signal — and the brief explicitly asks for visibility.
  • Safety floor: afterthought or headline? Both — hardcode the 30-min sprint, then demo an early unplug live. Trust is the adoption gate.
  • Scope. Vertical slice (A+B+D+E) vs “build the VPP dispatch layer”. The 48-hour clock decides; the grid endpoints stay as stubs that show the direction.

Blind spots caught in review

  • The draft demo paired a UK grid signal with California sessions. → Fixed: WattTime CAISO_NORTH (free Basic plan, 72 h forecast) + gridstatus.
  • LP infeasibility was unhandled. → Elastic constraints.
  • Liability: the optimiser throttles real current. → Hardware limits set outside the LP; software can only delay.
  • Unaudited “receipts” dressed as certificates are a credibility risk. → Labelled estimates; certificates are roadmap.
  • No feature-by-feature comparison with named incumbents. → §7.
  • Privacy of ISO 15118 / departure data. → §9.

Verdict

Build it — as a site-owner product for daytime lots, not a driver-facing green app. The first thing to do is not UI: run the elastic LP on 40 real ACN-Data sessions against PG&E BEV rates and WattTime CAISO_NORTH history and print the site’s bill and CO₂ delta versus charge-immediately. If that number is compelling, it is the pitch. If not, nothing else matters.

The trail

  1. Started from the brief and asked what “align with renewables” physically means → it is a timing problem, so the product is a scheduler.
  2. Looked for the behaviour data. Gupta et al. (Rivian telematics) gave the killer default-behaviour fact: 72% charge immediately, <26% schedule [1]. Any solution requiring driver effort was out.
  3. Looked for the grid-side data. EIA/CAISO: 3.4 TWh curtailed in 2024, midday, 93% solar [6]. So the clean hours are daytime.
  4. Asked where cars are in daytime. Powell et al. (Nature Energy 2022): shifting to daytime charging beats home-overnight on storage, curtailment, ramping and emissions [2]. Workplace dwell studies: 8–10 h parked for 7–10 kWh [18]. The wedge appeared.
  5. Mapped the incumbents. All the carbon-aware products live at home overnight; the workplace/CPO products optimise cost. Empty square.
  6. Found the technical constraint nobody has answered yet. Martin, Powell & Rajagopal (Dec 2025): broadcast emissions signals can increase emissions at scale; cascading group-wise signals fix it [3]. This is why the design optimises centrally per site and never broadcasts.
  7. Checked feasibility. OCPP 1.6/2.0.1 support charging profiles and (2.0.1) departure time [13]; WattTime’s free plan covers CAISO_NORTH with forecasts [8]; ACN-Data has 30 k real sessions [16]; PG&E’s BEV rate makes carbon and cost point the same way [12].
  8. Pressure-tested (§11) and simplified: dropped the lanes, made the LP elastic, fixed the signal/session grid mismatch, named the payer.

Assumptions we challenged

Everyone assumes…What we found
“Green = low average grid carbon”Decisions move the marginal generator; at scale even marginal signals fail if broadcast [3].
“Drivers must choose to charge green”72% never choose anything; the default decides [1].
“The problem is at home, overnight”The solar surplus and the idle plugged-in hours are both daytime, at work [2] [18].
“Price already encodes carbon”Most off-peak windows are overnight (gas). California’s BEV rate is the exception [12] [22].
“Departure time is unknowable”OCPP 2.0.1/ISO 15118 carry it; ACN has asked users for it since 2018; history predicts it well enough for a conservative default [13] [16].
“Per-session carbon accounting is hand-waving”Hourly granular certificates are now a standard with accredited issuers (June 2025) — a roadmap, not a fantasy [15].
